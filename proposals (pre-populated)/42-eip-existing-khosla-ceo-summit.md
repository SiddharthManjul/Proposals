CATEGORY: EIP
TITLE: Retire the panel format at the Khosla Ventures CEO Summit and replace with operator-led workshops

ABSTRACT: The Khosla Ventures CEO Summit gathers portfolio CEOs annually at a destination resort for three days of programming. The format is panel-and-speaker-heavy, mixing portfolio CEOs with celebrity speakers, policy figures and outside executives. The signal-to-noise ratio of this programming has eroded as the portfolio has grown and the speakers have rotated through what is by now a well-trodden circuit of similar VC summits. The wedge is to retire the panel-and-speaker format entirely and replace it with operator-led workshops on specific topics chosen by attendees in advance, with no outside speakers and no panels longer than thirty minutes. The summit becomes a working event rather than a content event.

BODY:
A CEO of a Khosla Ventures portfolio company who has been to three or four annual summits can recite the speaker rotation by heart. Same policy figures, same executive coaches, same prominent founders, same general partner panels. The content is genuinely good content, but it is the same content other VC summits also program, and the CEO has heard most of it before. The thing the CEO would find genuinely useful (a structured working session with three other portfolio CEOs who are stuck on the same operational problem this quarter) is not part of the program because the program is built around speakers and panels, not around peer work.

## What's wrong with the current format

The Khosla CEO Summit runs three days at a destination property, typically in late summer or early fall. By my count, the program contains roughly `~40 to 60 distinct sessions across three days`, including keynote speakers (`~6 to 10`), panels (`~12 to 18`), small breakouts (`~10 to 15`) and free networking time. Total attendees: `~200 to 300 portfolio CEOs and selected guests`. The format is well-organized, the venue is well-chosen, the food is excellent.

The structural issue is that the format optimizes for content density rather than for working time. A CEO at the summit who wants to actually solve a specific operational problem they are wrestling with has very little structured time to do that. They can have a fifteen-minute coffee conversation with a peer in the hallway between panels. They can squeeze in a thirty-minute working session in their hotel room before dinner. But the format itself does not allocate time to working, only to listening.

By my read of post-summit survey data and conversations with attendees, the part of the summit that consistently produces the most value is the unscheduled time: the dinner conversations, the early-morning runs, the bar after the formal programming. The structured programming produces moderate value. The panels in particular produce low value, because panels are inherently surface-level: four panelists with eight minutes each cannot say anything substantive about a specific operational problem.

The cost of the summit is significant: `~$1.5M to $3M annually` across venue, travel, speaker fees, programming, food, and ops. The cost per CEO-day of high-signal output is therefore relatively high, and could be substantially lower if the program were structured around the activities that actually produce value (working time, peer conversation) rather than around the activities that fill calendar slots (panels, keynotes).

Khosla is a fund that has historically been willing to question conventional VC practice. The CEO Summit is a piece of conventional VC practice that deserves the question.

## Proposed format

Restructure the CEO Summit into a working summit: operator-led workshops on specific operational topics chosen by attendees in advance, with no outside speakers and no panels longer than thirty minutes.

**Duration.** Three days, unchanged. Thursday afternoon arrival to Sunday morning departure.

**Program structure.** Each day organized around four ninety-minute workshop blocks. Each block runs three to five parallel workshops, each capped at fifteen to twenty CEOs. Workshops focus on specific operational topics submitted by attendees in advance. CEOs choose which workshops to attend.

**Workshop topics.** Submitted by attendees four weeks before the summit. Examples: "fundraising as we approach the cash-flow-positive inflection point", "managing the technical-debt curve at Series C", "the second-time CFO transition", "navigating a board chair who disagrees with the roadmap", "the eighteen-month operational pivot in vertical X". Topics are concrete and stage-specific. The program team curates the submitted topics into the final agenda based on attendee interest.

**Workshop format.** Each workshop has one or two attendee-CEOs as co-facilitators, chosen because they have direct operational experience with the topic. The format is structured peer discussion, not presentation. The co-facilitators do not lecture. They pose the question and steer the conversation. The other CEOs in the room contribute their experience. Ninety minutes is the cadence: enough time for real depth, not so much that energy flags.

**No outside speakers.** No celebrity speakers, no policy figures, no executive coaches, no journalists. The summit's content is generated by the attending CEOs. The only non-CEO presence is the Khosla partner team and a small operations crew.

**Panel limits.** A maximum of two panels in the entire three-day program. Each panel runs thirty minutes. Three to four panelists. Topic chosen for breadth rather than depth.

**Host shape.** Khosla partners attend as participants. The two or three Khosla partners most involved with the summit attend each workshop block, mostly as observers and contributors rather than as program leads. No partner-led mainstage time.

**Off-the-record.** Chatham House throughout. Names not quotable. Substance discussable. The off-the-record rule is what makes the workshops produce real value rather than rehearsed talking points.

**Success criterion.** Six months after each summit, attendee survey on whether at least three specific operational decisions in the attendee's company were materially influenced by a summit workshop conversation. Floor: `~60% of attendees` report at least three. Stretch: `~80%`. Plus peer-relationship persistence at six months: floor `>40%` of attendees report at least two ongoing peer conversations from the summit.

**Kill criterion.** Two consecutive summits where attendee NPS falls below `50` or where peer-relationship-persistence falls below `~25%`. Indicates the workshop format is not delivering. Re-examine workshop curation before retiring the format.

## Mechanics

- **Workshop topic submission.** Four weeks before the summit. Attendees submit one to three topics they want to discuss. Topics scored for breadth of interest (how many other attendees indicated interest in the same area). Top topics become workshops.
- **Co-facilitator selection.** For each workshop, the program team identifies one or two attending CEOs with direct operational experience on the topic, and asks them to co-facilitate. Co-facilitators do brief preparation (`~2 hours`) but not extensive deck-building.
- **Workshop scheduling.** Four ninety-minute blocks per day, three to five parallel workshops per block. Twelve workshop slots per day, thirty-six over the three days, supporting roughly `~80 to 150 different topic permutations` across the program.
- **Venue.** Same destination property as current. The format change does not require venue change.
- **Budget.** Workshop format saves on speaker fees (current speakers typically receive `~$25K to $75K each`, eliminating these saves `~$200K to $500K annually`). Operational complexity is comparable. Net cost: roughly flat or modestly lower than the current format.
- **Attendee distribution.** Mix of attendees across workshop blocks managed by the program. CEOs cannot all attend the same hot workshop. Structure encourages diverse cross-cohort exposure.
- **Documentation.** Light. One paragraph per workshop summarizing themes (not attributions), shared with attendees only. No external publication. No marketing extraction.
- **Smallest viable version.** Run a single workshop track for half a day at the existing summit format in year one (alongside the legacy panel program). Compare attendee value scores between the two halves. Scale to the full workshop format the following year if the workshop half scores meaningfully higher.

## Risks

**Risk: portfolio CEOs miss the celebrity-speaker access that the legacy format provided.** Mitigation: the legacy format's celebrity-speaker access was always thin (a brief stage appearance, no real interaction). Replacing it with deeper peer conversation is a trade most CEOs will value once they experience it. The first year of transition may produce some grumbling. By year two, the grumbling settles.

**Risk: workshop quality varies widely across topics and some workshops produce weak conversation.** Mitigation: co-facilitator selection is the highest-leverage decision. Good co-facilitators produce good workshops. The program team builds a roster of past co-facilitators rated by attendees and uses the rating in future years.

**Risk: the summit loses its "we hosted X famous person" marketing dimension.** Mitigation: the summit was never primarily a marketing event. The brand value of the summit comes from the portfolio CEO cohort and the partner attention, both of which are preserved. The marketing dimension that does get lost is one Khosla can afford to lose given the rest of the firm's brand presence.

**Risk: the workshop format excludes non-CEO portfolio executives who might benefit (CFOs, CTOs).** Mitigation: a "functional leader day" before or after the CEO summit, structured the same way but for functional cohorts. Adds one to two days of programming for the function-specific cohorts.

**Risk: attendees who joined the summit primarily for the speaker access (or for the perceived "premium event" feel) stop attending.** Mitigation: this is partly the goal. The summit's value proposition shifts to peer learning, which is what the format actually delivers. Attendees who valued only the legacy positioning self-select out. The cohort tightens.

## Open questions

How does this format interact with the Khosla partners' own outreach to specific portfolio CEOs at the summit? Probably positively. Partners who previously spent time on stage or backstage now spend time in workshop discussions with their portfolio CEOs as peers. The partner-CEO relationship deepens through working time together.

What about partner-led "office hours" at the summit? Continue them in parallel. Office hours are good and the workshop format does not replace them. Add scheduled partner office hours during the free portions of the day.

How does this affect attendee attendance at all? Probably mixed in year one (some attendees decline because they preferred the legacy format), stable or improving by year three. The right comparison is value-per-attendee, not raw attendance count.

Should the summit grow beyond three hundred attendees if the workshop format works well? Probably not. The peer-learning premise requires that any given workshop room has at most fifteen to twenty CEOs. Scaling beyond three hundred attendees requires either reducing workshop interaction depth or adding parallel workshop tracks past the point where attendees feel they are missing valuable parallel sessions. Three hundred is roughly the right ceiling.

What about the prospective-CEO and external-attendee guest list that the legacy summit included? Probably reduce. The workshop format works best when every attendee is a portfolio CEO. Prospective founders and external guests fit awkwardly. A small number of explicit "alumni" attendees (CEOs of Khosla portfolio companies that have exited) can fit because they share the peer history. Outside-portfolio guests should be rare.
