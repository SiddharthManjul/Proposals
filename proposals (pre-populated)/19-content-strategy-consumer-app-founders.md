CATEGORY: CIP
TITLE: Earn ten thousand users from one community before scaling to paid platforms

ABSTRACT: Consumer and prosumer app founders default to TikTok, Reels and YouTube Shorts to drive installs. Cost per install has risen `3 to 5x` since 2023 as the platforms compressed organic reach, and the typical CPI for an indie consumer app now sits above what early monetization can support. The wedge for the first ten thousand users is not paid distribution. It is one micro-community where the founder is a known, useful, non-promotional participant for six to twelve months. The product launches into the community after credibility is established, not before.

BODY:
The first ten thousand users of a consumer app are not the same audience as the next million. The first ten thousand are early adopters with a specific shape: they belong to communities, they argue about products in those communities and they refer to friends inside those communities when something is genuinely good. The "growth team" mindset that works at scale (paid social, A/B-tested onboarding, viral loops) does not apply to acquiring the first ten thousand, because the first ten thousand do not respond to ads from companies they have never heard of.

## What's wrong now

The default playbook for a consumer-app founder at the pre-Seed and Seed stage looks like this: build the MVP, set up a website with a waitlist, run a `$5K to $15K` paid test on TikTok and Instagram, hire a part-time growth marketer to optimize the funnel. The numbers are bad and getting worse.

Cost per install for a consumer app at the indie-app price point ranges from `$4` for a low-competition vertical to `$25 to $45` for any category with established players (productivity, social, fitness, dating). Conversion from install to retained-user-at-day-30 sits between `8 and 18%` for most apps, which means the true cost per retained user is `$25 to $200+`. At those numbers, the LTV math does not work for an app monetizing at `$5 to $15/month` until the product is far more developed than at first launch.

Worse, the paid-acquisition motion teaches the founder nothing about the user. Ad-installed users do not give feedback. They churn silently. The founder ends up with a thousand-user product, no roadmap signal and a bank balance burning toward the next round.

By contrast, founders who acquire their first cohort from one community report `40 to 70%` day-30 retention, organic word-of-mouth referral rates above `15%` and a roadmap that is largely written by user feedback inside the community itself. The audience is smaller in absolute terms but qualitatively different.

## The proposed change

Replace the paid-acquisition motion (for the pre-product-market-fit phase) with a community-residency strategy. Identify one online community where the target user is genuinely active. Spend six to twelve months as a useful, non-promotional contributor before launching the product to the community. Use the community's feedback to shape the product roadmap. Launch publicly only after the community has been told the product is coming.

The "community" here is specific. Not a generic startup-Reddit or product-Hunt audience. A vertical or identity community where the founder has genuine domain interest. Examples: a specific Discord server for a fandom or hobby, a niche subreddit (`r/CompetitiveOverwatch`, `r/lockpicking`, `r/MarchAgainstNazis`), a long-running forum (Hackaday, Reef2Reef, EEVblog) or a specific industry's Slack workspace. The qualifying property is: the founder personally wants to be there even if they were not building a product.

What changes versus the paid-acquisition default: the channel (one community vs many ad surfaces), the timeline (six months of presence before launch vs an immediate paid push), the founder's role (contributor first, then product person) and the early-cohort retention shape (high because acquired users self-selected for fit).

## Mechanics

- **Audit candidate communities.** List 5 to 10 communities where the target user might be. Eliminate any where founder/marketer presence is obvious and shunned (most active subreddits, certain Discord servers with strict no-promotion rules). Eliminate any where the founder does not have personal domain interest. Pick the one remaining where the founder can genuinely contribute for six months without being bored.
- **Presence cadence.** `5 to 10` substantive contributions per week (substantive: answering a question well, sharing a build, asking a real question of your own). No product mentions for the first three months. The fourth-month bar for any product mention: another community member asks something directly relevant and the product is the honest answer.
- **Launch sequencing.** Once the product is at MVP, post an honest, full-disclosure launch in the community. Example phrasing: *"I've been a member here since March. The thing I built started because of [specific community thread I participated in]. Here's what it does, here's where it falls short and here's how to try it."* The community will either reject the launch (rare if the prior six months were genuine) or carry it on their own channels (common).
- **Feedback loop.** Designate a community-specific channel (a dedicated thread, a Discord channel, a Twitter list) where feedback from this cohort flows back to the product team within `48 hours`. The cohort knows their feedback shapes the roadmap. They watch for changes. They evangelize when they see their suggestions ship.
- **Cost.** Founder time, `5 to 10 hours/week` for the residency. Zero paid acquisition during this phase. Optional: `$500/month` if a paid-membership community (some Discord and Slack groups are paid).
- **Smallest viable version.** Reduce to one substantive post per week for three months. If the response signal is positive, scale up. If the community ignores the founder entirely, the community was the wrong choice.
- **Traction metric.** Community-member conversion to product user at launch. Floor: `10%` of active community members try the product within `30 days` of launch. Stretch: `25%`.
- **Retention metric.** Day-30 retention of the community-acquired cohort. Floor: `40%`. Stretch: `60%+`. Compare to the same metric for any paid-acquisition cohort once paid is reintroduced.

## Open questions

What is the right ratio of community-time to product-build-time? Founders cannot do both fully. My read is that the residency works at roughly `15 to 25%` of the founder's time, alongside product building, for six months. More than `25%` and the product slips. Less than `15%` and the contributions are not enough to build credibility.

What happens if the launch is rejected by the community? Rare when the residency is genuine, but not impossible. The recovery is to take the feedback seriously, fix the rejected aspect and re-launch a different version six months later, after rebuilding trust. This is slow. It is also a lesson worth more than the cost.

After the first ten thousand users from one community, what does the founder do? My instinct is to find another community with the same shape, not to scale into paid acquisition immediately. The reason: the community-acquired cohort gives the product the early reputation it needs in order for later paid-acquisition CPMs to be lower than market rate, because creative-side performance and retention math both improve.

How does this generalize across different product categories? Productivity, social, fitness, dating, gaming and creative-tool apps all have community-resident strategies that work. Pure utility apps (a calculator, a flashlight) typically do not, but pure utility apps usually do not need this strategy anyway because their distribution is search and app-store-feature, not community-driven.

Does this strategy depend on founder authenticity? Yes, and it cannot be faked. Founders who try to do this without genuine domain interest get caught inside two months. The community spots performative engagement. If the founder cannot honestly say *"I would be in this community even if I were not building a product"*, the strategy is the wrong one for that team.
