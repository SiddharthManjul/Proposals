CATEGORY: CMIP
TITLE: Stand up a customer advisory board with rotating seats and Chatham House minutes

ABSTRACT: B2B SaaS founders default to a vendor-controlled Slack or forum that customers do not trust because every post becomes a marketing opportunity. The wedge is a customer advisory board with rotating seats, term limits, published minutes redacted to Chatham House rules and a clear separation between vendor-staff participation and customer participation. Customers will speak honestly to each other when the format protects them, and the founder learns more from one well-run advisory board than from ten customer-success calls.

BODY:
B2B SaaS founders ask their customers two kinds of questions. The first is "would you renew?" The second is "what should we build next?" Both questions get answers, but the answers are bad. The customer who is on a quarterly business review with their account executive will not say "we are looking at your competitor" and will not say "the feature you are about to ship is the wrong one" because the conversation is recorded, the vendor controls the room and the customer's leverage is preserved by hedging. The founder ends up with a roadmap built on signals from customers who were managing the relationship, not telling the truth.

## What's broken

The default B2B SaaS customer community is one of two shapes. The first is a vendor-controlled Slack workspace where the company posts product updates, the customer-success team monitors channels and customers occasionally ask questions that company employees answer within an hour. The vendor's presence dominates. Customers do not talk to each other in this format because everything they say is read by the vendor. By my count, in a typical vendor-Slack with `~300 customer-company members`, customer-to-customer posts run at `~5%` of total volume. The rest is vendor-to-customer or customer-to-vendor.

The second shape is a periodic customer advisory call: quarterly, on Zoom, run by product management, attended by twenty customers who watched a slide deck and asked three questions. The signal-to-noise ratio of these calls is low. The customers who attend are the ones who say yes to vendor meetings. Their feedback is correlated with their willingness to attend vendor meetings, not with the broader customer base's needs.

Neither shape produces honest customer-to-customer learning. Neither shape produces the strategic signal the founder thinks it produces. The customer churn that surprised the founder last quarter was probably visible in the customer's own conversations with peers, which the founder cannot hear.

## Proposed change

Stand up a customer advisory board with the governance shape of a public-sector advisory committee. Specific structure:

**Seven to nine customer seats.** Selected to represent the diversity of the customer base: company size, vertical, geography, length of relationship. Not the most-loved customers exclusively. Include at least two customers who have recently expressed dissatisfaction.

**Twelve-month terms, single-renewal limit.** A given customer can serve `24 consecutive months` maximum. After that they rotate off. Staggered: roughly half the seats turn over every six months.

**Three or four quarterly meetings per year.** Two-hour sessions. Half on customer-to-customer peer discussion (the founder and vendor staff present but participating minimally), half on vendor-led roadmap discussion.

**Chatham House rules in the customer-to-customer half.** Specific customers cannot be quoted by name outside the meeting. The substance of the discussion can be discussed publicly with attribution removed. This protects customers from competitive exposure and from looking disloyal to their own organization.

**Published minutes.** Within `7 days` of each meeting, redacted minutes go to the full advisory board and (in further-redacted form) to the broader customer community as a quarterly product report. Names removed. Themes preserved.

**Vendor-staff role distinct from peer role.** Founder and product leaders attend in a "facilitator and learner" role, not as full participants. Vendor staff do not vote, do not steer customer-to-customer discussion and do not respond to customer concerns in real-time during the customer-to-customer half. Responses come back in writing within `14 days`.

What changes versus today: the seat structure (representative, rotated, not self-selected), the format (peer-to-peer half is honest because vendor is not in the conversation), the artifacts (minutes are the record, not the customer-success team's notes) and the founder's role (facilitator, not the center of the meeting).

## What this is and isn't

This is a strategic listening mechanism with formal governance, modeled on public-sector advisory committees and physician-society practice. It is not a customer-marketing program and the advisory board members are not testimonials. The Chatham House protection only works if it is respected. A founder who quotes an advisory board member in a marketing deck three months later breaks the protection and the board's value collapses inside one cycle.

This is also not a vote on product direction. The customer advisory board's authority is recommendation-shaped. The founder retains final call on the roadmap and the commercial strategy. The board's role is to surface concerns the founder would not otherwise hear, to surface needs the customer-success funnel filters out and to validate or contest the founder's strategic instincts. It is not the product committee.

This is not a substitute for the customer-success function. The board is for strategic listening. Day-to-day customer-success operations (renewals, escalations, training) continue through the existing channels. The board's role is the layer above, the layer that informs strategy.

## Mechanics

- **Selection.** First seats: founder-appointed from the existing customer base, with explicit diversity criteria (size, vertical, geography, relationship length, including at least two recently dissatisfied). Subsequent seats: nomination from current board members plus self-nomination, decided by board vote with founder confirmation.
- **Term length.** Twelve months, single renewal. Maximum `24 consecutive months` per customer. Staggered.
- **Recusal.** Customers recuse from discussions of features they are direct competitors of. Customers in active escalations recuse from those specific discussions.
- **Time commitment.** Eight hours per quarter per customer: two-hour meeting, two hours of pre-read, four hours of follow-up. Sit at four meetings per year.
- **Compensation.** No payment (compensation creates conflict-of-interest and the board's value is in disinterested judgment). Travel covered for any in-person meetings. Free access to the product's highest tier during the term. Public credit on the company's "Customer Advisory Board" page (with the customer's permission).
- **Vendor-staff attendance.** Founder, head of product, head of customer success. Other staff by invitation. Maximum four vendor staff per meeting to avoid outnumbering customer participation.
- **Minutes.** Drafted by the board's chair (a customer member, elected by the board) with founder-team review for accuracy, not for editing. Published to the full board within `7 days`. Published in further-redacted summary form to the broader customer community quarterly.
- **Smallest viable version.** Skip the formal governance for the first cycle. Start with five customers, two meetings, light minutes. If the second meeting produces honest strategic feedback the founder did not have before, formalize the structure. If the meetings stay polite, the format was wrong (probably too much vendor presence).
- **Traction metric.** Number of strategic decisions (product, commercial, partnership) materially influenced by board feedback per year. Floor: `2 per year`. Stretch: `6+`. If zero in year one, the board is decorative.
- **Retention metric.** Board attendance rate. Healthy: `>85%` of seats present at each meeting. Below `70%` is the warning sign that the board format is not delivering value to members and they are deprioritizing it.

## Open questions

Should the broader customer community see the redacted summary or only the board? My read is yes, the broader community should see a quarterly summary because the transparency creates demand to join the board and validates the board's existence. The risk is that customers outside the board feel excluded. Mitigation is making the nomination process public.

Does this work for smaller customer bases (under `~50 customers`)? Less well. With small customer bases, individual customer conversations are already high-bandwidth and the formal structure adds overhead without changing the signal. The board format starts paying off around `~100 to 200 paying customers`. Smaller companies should run informal "founders' lunches" with three or four customers at a time on a rotating basis instead.

How does the board interact with the customer-success team's existing advocacy programs? Carefully. The board members are likely to overlap with customer advocates. The board's role is strategic. Advocates are tactical. Confusion between the two roles creates either marketing-flavored board meetings (bad) or strategy-loaded advocacy programs (also bad). Keep the roles separated and tell members which role they are playing in which meeting.

What happens when the board's recommendation conflicts with the founder's roadmap? The founder explains the conflict in writing within `14 days` and decides. The board's authority is recommendation-shaped. Repeated conflicts where the founder consistently overrides the board are a warning sign that the board's composition is wrong, not that the board is wrong. Reconsider the seats before reconsidering the structure.

How does this scale past `~500 customers`? Most boards stay at seven to nine seats regardless of customer-base size, because larger groups stop producing honest peer-to-peer conversation. Larger customer bases need parallel boards (by segment, by vertical) rather than larger boards.
