CATEGORY: EIP
TITLE: Run monthly no-slides demo nights where founders show working software for ten minutes

ABSTRACT: Standard demo nights produce founder polish, not founder capability. Slides, projections and team-bio slides let a founder present a company that does not yet meaningfully work. A demo night with strict constraints (working software shown live, ten minutes per founder, no slides, no projections, no team bios) selects for founders whose product is already real and produces an evening of unusually high signal density. The format is also unusual in that the host VC's audience grows on its own: technical operators and engineers attend specifically because the format filters out the noise they hate.

BODY:
The traditional demo night is a hybrid event. Half pitch, half product showcase, with the founder controlling the deck and the demo. The format flatters founders whose product is presentable but not necessarily real. By removing slides and projections, the demo night reverts to its original purpose: see whether the thing works. The format is older than venture capital and the constraints are exactly what makes it useful.

## What's wrong with the current format

Walk into any demo night put on by an accelerator or fund in 2026. Five to ten founders take the stage. Each has a deck. The deck has a hero slide, a problem slide, a solution slide, a team slide, a traction slide, an ask. The "demo" is a screenshare of the product, often pre-recorded or carefully rehearsed. The audience watches `~5 to 8 founders` over `~90 minutes` and remembers `~2` afterward.

The format selects for slide-making skill. It does not select for product capability. By my count, in a random sample of fifteen demo nights I have attended in the last eighteen months, the median founder's slide deck was substantially more polished than their product. Some products that looked great on the slide did not work when I asked the founder to show me later. Some founders whose decks were rough had products that were already deployed to paying customers and were impressive when I sat with them and watched the thing run.

The format also selects for founders who can perform on stage, which is loosely correlated with the ability to recruit and sell but uncorrelated with the ability to build. The conversion math is consistent with this. By my read of post-event tracking from three accelerator demo nights, roughly `~1 to 2 investments per event` materialize, out of `~50 to 80 investor-attendees per event`. The conversion rate per investor-hour is low and the selection signal is unclear.

Worse, the audience the host most wants in the room (operators, technical evaluators, the senior engineers who could become founders or could be hired by founders) increasingly skip these events. They have learned that the signal-to-noise is bad and they spend their evenings elsewhere. The demo night format has shed the audience that made it valuable.

## Proposed format

A monthly demo night with strict format constraints, hosted by the fund, designed to filter for product capability and to attract the technical audience that has stopped attending standard demo nights.

**Duration.** Two and a half hours total. Six founders, ten minutes each, equals sixty minutes of demos. Twenty-minute Q&A with the audience after each demo block of two. Thirty minutes of structured networking after.

**Constraints on the demo.** Working software. Shown live on the founder's own laptop, projected onto the venue's screen, but no slide decks. No PowerPoint, Keynote, Google Slides, Figma frames or pre-recorded video. No "team" slide, no "market opportunity" slide, no "traction" slide. The founder talks while the product runs. If the product crashes during the demo, the founder debugs in real time. The audience is told this rule explicitly so they know what they are watching.

**Cadence.** Monthly. One demo night per month in one city (typically the host fund's home city, occasionally rotating).

**Composition.** Six founders per night, selected by application. Audience is `~40 to 60` people, mix of operators, engineers, junior VCs and prospective founders. No press unless explicitly approved by the founder being demoed.

**Host shape.** A single partner from the fund hosts. The host introduces each founder in thirty seconds (name, company, one-sentence description) and then steps back. The host does not present, does not pitch the fund and does not sit on a panel.

**Q&A format.** After each pair of demos, twenty minutes of open Q&A. Questions come from the audience. The host moderates lightly. The first question to each founder is "what does not work yet?" The founder must answer honestly. The remaining questions are open.

**Success criterion.** Twelve months after each cohort of six, what fraction have either raised a round (any fund) or are at material revenue that suggests they will not need to raise? Floor: `60%`. Stretch: `80%`. The format is high-signal so the floor is high.

**Kill criterion.** Three consecutive months with audience attendance below `25` and applicant pool below `15` (for six slots). Indicates the format has lost its audience or its applicant pool, both of which mean the event is not delivering what it should.

## Mechanics

- **Venue.** A space with a projector, a stable internet connection and seating for fifty. A coworking space or a tech-friendly bar with private space works well. Same venue per month if possible.
- **Budget.** `~$2K to $4K per night`. Includes venue, light food, drinks. Annual cost: `~$30 to $45K`. Modest.
- **Application form.** Open on the fund's website. Founders submit a video of the working product (`~3 minutes`, screen recording, no slides), a description of what they want to demo and one specific question they want from the audience. Reviewed by an associate plus the host partner. Selection criteria: working software (verified by the submitted video), interesting product, willingness to show what does not work as well as what does.
- **Selection panel.** Two people from the host fund review applications. Disagreement resolved by the host partner. Decisions made one to two weeks before the event.
- **Founder briefing.** The host calls each selected founder one week before to confirm format rules. The rules are repeated in writing. Founders who attempt to bring slides on the night are politely turned away with the slot reallocated to a backup.
- **Format enforcement.** The host or a designated stagehand operates the projector. The projector connects only to the founder's laptop, not to a presentation app. If a founder's laptop boots into a slide deck, the demo is paused and the deck is closed.
- **Audience filtering.** Public sign-up form for attendees, with a short statement of who they are and why they want to attend. Capacity capped at sixty. First-come-first-served among approved attendees. The fund's portfolio CEOs and engineering leads get a standing invitation.
- **Documentation.** Internal one-paragraph note per demo, written within forty-eight hours by the host partner. No external publication or recording.

## Risks

**Risk: founders cannot demo for ten minutes without slides.** Mitigation: the application form requires evidence of demo-readiness (the three-minute video). Founders who cannot produce a no-slides video are not invited. The first time a founder forgets and tries to show a slide, the host firmly redirects. The format teaches itself within two or three events.

**Risk: the demos crash live and the founder is humiliated.** Mitigation: the format makes crashes okay. The host's opening framing explicitly says "if it crashes, debug in front of us. That is interesting too." Some of the strongest signal comes from how a founder handles a real crash. Founders who cannot handle it gracefully signal something the format is designed to surface.

**Risk: the format selects against founders whose products are not yet demoable (regulated products, hardware in pre-launch, deeply backend infrastructure with no UI).** Mitigation: a separate "non-visual demo" track allows founders to bring three minutes of structured talk plus working data or working benchmarks rather than UI. Same constraints (no slides, no projections, no team bios). Different surface area, similar selection signal.

**Risk: the question "what does not work yet?" puts founders in an awkward position with investors in the room.** Mitigation: the question is a feature, not a bug. The honest answer is usually impressive. The founder who cannot answer it well is the founder the audience needed to see.

**Risk: a presenting founder is harmed by an investor who saw the demo and uses the information against them.** Mitigation: the audience composition is curated. Public application and host-approval. Repeat investors who behave badly are not invited back. The format depends on a healthy audience norm and the host enforces it.

## Open questions

How does the format work for funds whose home cities lack a deep technical audience? Less well. The format depends on an audience of operators and engineers who can ask substantive Q&A questions. In smaller cities, the format can still run but the audience size and quality is lower. The founder-side selection signal remains high but the operator-side learning suffers.

What about hardware demos and other formats that need physical space or special equipment? The constraints relax appropriately. A hardware founder can bring a prototype and show it work. A robotics demo can use the venue floor. The principle is consistency: show the thing working, not the slide describing what the thing should do.

How does the format scale beyond one city? Each city can run its own monthly demo night with the same constraints. Cross-city coordination is light. The format is locally executed. A multi-city fund can run three or four demo nights per month total without diluting the brand.

When does the format mature? Probably after twelve to eighteen monthly events in one city. The audience develops norms, the applicant pool develops a reputation among founders, the conversion rate stabilizes. After two years, the format becomes a known and trusted institution in the local builder community.

Should the demos be public after the night, recorded and posted? Probably not. The trust between founder and audience depends on the demo not being a marketing event. Recording changes the founder's behavior and dilutes the signal. The format is private by design, even when the audience is large.
