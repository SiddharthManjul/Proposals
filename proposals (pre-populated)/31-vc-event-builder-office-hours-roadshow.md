CATEGORY: EIP
TITLE: Run a quarterly roadshow of builder office hours across four regional tech hubs

ABSTRACT: Sand Hill office hours select for founders who can afford the Sand Hill pilgrimage, which biases against founders who are too deep in building to travel. The VC that wants to source the best founders before competitors notice them should bring office hours to where the builders are. A quarterly roadshow visiting four regional tech hubs (typically two of: Boston, Pittsburgh, Austin, Toronto, Boulder, Seattle, Atlanta, plus one international city) with twenty thirty-minute slots per visit, no pre-meeting deck, no panel, just direct conversation. The format selects for builders who would not otherwise be in the VC's pipeline and produces a higher hit rate than the average sourcing channel.

BODY:
The strongest predictor of a VC's three-year IRR is access to founders before they are widely discovered. Every fund knows this. Most funds invest enormous partner time in the Sand Hill office-hours format, which optimizes for founders willing to travel to Sand Hill and against founders too deep in building to spend a day on a plane. The founders who are most deeply at work are the ones most likely to be missed.

## What's wrong with the current format

The default sourcing motion for an early-stage fund includes office hours at the fund's headquarters. Partners block roughly one day per month for these. Each slot runs thirty minutes. The fund hears `~8 to 12 founders per office-hours day`, sourced through warm introductions and a public sign-up form. The founders who attend are disproportionately Bay Area or NYC based, disproportionately already in the fund's existing network and disproportionately at a stage where they are actively raising.

The conversion math for office hours of this shape sits at roughly `~1 investment per 40 to 60 office-hour conversations`. The cost of partner time per investment runs `~$60K to $100K` in fully-loaded fund cost. The investments that come from this channel are reasonable but not differentiated from inbound or warm-intro sourcing. The fund is paying partner time to filter founders who would have reached the fund anyway through other channels.

The harder problem is the founders the fund never hears from. By my read of the geographic distribution of recent seed-stage software startups, roughly `~55 to 65%` are headquartered outside the Bay Area or New York. Of those, roughly half are in cities that have a real builder community (Boston, Pittsburgh, Austin, Toronto, Boulder, Seattle, Atlanta, plus the international cities). These founders are reachable by a fund that goes to them. They are mostly invisible to a fund that does not.

## Proposed format

A quarterly roadshow of builder office hours across four cities per cycle. The fund's partners travel to each city, hold structured one-on-one office hours over two days per city, then return to base.

**Duration per city.** Two days. Approximately sixteen thirty-minute slots per partner per city, split across the two days with reasonable breaks.

**Cadence.** Quarterly. Four cycles per year. Each cycle visits four cities. Total: sixteen city-visits per year. Each city is visited at most twice per year. Some cities cycle out after one year and new cities cycle in.

**City selection.** Each cycle should mix one "anchor" city (consistent across years, like Boston or Austin) with three rotating cities. International rotation: one of the four cities per cycle should be outside the United States (London, Toronto, Berlin, Tel Aviv, Bangalore, depending on the fund's geographic mandate).

**Office-hours format.** Thirty-minute slots. No pre-meeting deck required. Founders submit a short application describing what they are building (two paragraphs maximum) and a single specific question they have for the partner. The partner reads the application beforehand and arrives prepared to engage on that question. No formal pitch. No projector. No slides.

**Host shape.** Two partners per city when possible (allows parallel office hours and a second-partner sanity check on promising founders). One partner if scheduling requires.

**Success criterion.** Twelve months after each cycle, what fraction of office-hour attendees have either become a portfolio investment or raised from a peer fund where the host fund was the warm introduction? Floor: `8%`. Stretch: `18%`. Below `5%` the format is not generating sufficient signal.

**Kill criterion.** Two consecutive cycles in which a specific city produces zero investments and the second partner reports flat signal quality. Drop that city from rotation.

## Mechanics

- **Application.** Public form on the fund's website. Application opens four weeks before each cycle. Reviewed by an associate or junior partner. Roughly `~200 to 400 applications` per cycle, depending on the fund's brand and the cities visited. Selection criteria: a real company (not idea-stage), some demonstrated traction or technical depth, a specific question the partner can engage with usefully.
- **Slot scheduling.** Booked into a private room at a coworking space or a partner office in each city. The same venue per city per cycle (consistency reduces logistics).
- **Travel and venue budget.** `~$5K to $8K per city-visit` covering flights, hotel, ground transport, room rental and meals. Annual budget for the program: `~$80K to $130K`. Lower than running one large flagship event per year.
- **Partner time.** Roughly two travel days plus two office-hour days per city, so eight partner-days per cycle, or thirty-two partner-days per year. Comparable to or less than what most funds spend on speaking engagements and panels.
- **Local presence.** Coordinate with the local accelerator (Y Combinator, Techstars, MassChallenge, Founder Institute or local equivalents) and the local university entrepreneurship program. Not as a sponsor, but as a partner whose visit is announced to their network. Half of high-signal applications often come through this channel.
- **No public speaking.** Partners explicitly do not give talks or sit on panels during these visits. The visit is for one-on-one conversations only. Public speaking dilutes the format and changes the kind of founder who applies.
- **Follow-up.** Each office-hour conversation gets a written follow-up email from the partner within seven days, even when the answer is no. The follow-up names what was specifically interesting and what would change the partner's mind. Most founders who attend office hours and do not get invested in should feel that the conversation was worth their time.

## Risks

**Risk: the application form attracts a high volume of low-signal applications.** Mitigation: require the specific question and the two-paragraph product description. Filter applications that read like marketing copy. Reject ones that do not name something concrete the partner can engage with.

**Risk: partner travel fatigue.** Mitigation: cap city-days per partner at `~25 per year`. Rotate which partners attend each cycle. Junior partners can take some city-visits as professional development. The format is reasonably scalable across partner seniority.

**Risk: the format becomes seen as a "tier 2" sourcing channel and the best founders avoid it.** Mitigation: the office hours need to produce real conversations and visible follow-ups. Founders who get invested in from the office-hour channel should be celebrated internally and externally (with their permission). Word travels.

**Risk: a city visit yields no investments year after year and the partner team loses motivation to keep going.** Mitigation: the kill criterion is explicit. Drop cities that do not produce after two cycles. Add new cities in their place. The annual rotation prevents calcification.

**Risk: applicants in cities not visited feel excluded and treat the fund as Bay Area-only.** Mitigation: the public website lists upcoming cities and accepts applications for "no upcoming visit" with the understanding that the partner will reach out if the application is exceptional. The fund's reputation grows over time as the geographic coverage compounds.

## Open questions

How does this interact with the fund's existing geographic mandate? A pure Bay Area fund probably should not run this format. A fund with a real interest in distributed founders should run it as a core sourcing channel rather than a side experiment. The format is most effective when it represents an actual fund posture, not a marketing gesture.

What about international cities? Travel cost and time double for international visits, but the conversion rate per visit is often higher because the local VC competition is thinner. The fund should run at least one international city per year as a test, then expand based on signal.

How does the partner know which founder is worth following up with? The same way they know in any office-hours conversation. The advantage of this format is volume of conversations and selection of attendees who would not otherwise be in the pipeline. Signal quality on a per-conversation basis is similar to Sand Hill office hours. The source funnel is what differs.

What happens when the same founder attends multiple roadshow cycles? Allowed but tracked. Repeat attendees who get follow-ups and never get investment are signaling either that the founder is not the right fit for the fund (in which case the partner should be honest and say so) or that the partner is being too hesitant on a real opportunity (in which case the partner should re-examine).

Does the format work for stages above seed? Less well. By Series A, the founders are typically already in the fund's network through their seed-stage investors. The format is most powerful at the seed and pre-seed level where the founder is still mostly invisible to mainstream sourcing channels.
