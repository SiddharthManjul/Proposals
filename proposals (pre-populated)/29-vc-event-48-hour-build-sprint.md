CATEGORY: EIP
TITLE: Host a 48-hour build sprint where founders ship and the VC observes the work

ABSTRACT: VC sourcing events are dominated by speakers, panels and networking, which optimize for founder polish rather than founder capability. The best signal a VC can collect on a pre-seed or seed-stage founder is not how they pitch but how they work. A 48-hour build sprint, held in person, with twenty to thirty founders shipping visible progress on the product they are already building, lets the VC observe the thing that actually predicts the next five years. The format works because the question being answered (can this team build?) is the question that pitch meetings systematically fail to answer.

BODY:
The VC pitch meeting is a thirty-minute conversation in which the founder controls the slide deck, the room and the narrative arc. The VC asks questions, the founder answers them and the VC walks away with an impression of the founder's storytelling under pressure. None of that impression predicts whether the founder can ship product, recruit engineers, debug a customer call gone sideways or hold a roadmap together over twenty-four months. The best VCs know this. The best VCs invest anyway because the alternative (judging founders on actual work) takes more time than the pitch meeting takes and the sourcing funnel does not have room for it.

## What's wrong with the current format

The standard VC sourcing event is a half-day or full-day gathering in a downtown hotel or office. A typical event has `~120 to 250 attendees`, `~6 to 12` speakers including the VC's own partners, `~3 to 4` panels and a long networking break. The VC's partners give the keynote. Portfolio CEOs sit on panels. Prospective founders attend, take notes, exchange business cards and leave with the sense that they spent a day learning about the VC's brand. The VC leaves having met `~20 to 40 founders` in `~2-minute conversations`, of whom they remember `~5 to 8` by name a week later.

The conversion math is bad. By my read of partner-time-vs-deal-source-attribution data at three mid-stage funds I have spoken with, these events generate `~1 to 3 investments per year per fund` while consuming `~2 to 4 weeks of partner time` in planning and attendance. The cost per sourced investment runs `~$80K to $200K` in fully-loaded fund cost. The retention of these investments (do they reach Series A?) is roughly indistinguishable from cold-inbound or warm-intro sourced investments. The events generate brand impressions, which matter, but the brand impression could be generated by content for a tenth of the cost.

The harder failure is the signal quality. A founder who pitches well at a sourcing event might still be unable to ship product six months later. The VC has no way to know this from the pitch interaction. The pitch is theatre. The work is the work.

## Proposed format

A 48-hour in-person build sprint, hosted by the VC's partners, attended by twenty to thirty founders, who spend the weekend shipping visible progress on the products they are already building.

**Duration.** Friday evening 6pm to Sunday evening 6pm. Forty-eight hours. Founders can leave to sleep but the venue is open the whole time.

**Cadence.** Quarterly. Four per year, rotating across two cities (typically SF and NYC, occasionally adding LA or Boston).

**Constraints.** Founders attend solo or as founding teams of up to three. They must be working on a real company (not a hackathon idea). They must ship something visible by Sunday 6pm: a feature, a benchmark, a customer integration, an open-source release, a working demo of a new flow. The shipped thing must be public by Sunday 6pm.

**Host shape.** Two of the VC's partners attend the full weekend. They do not speak from a stage. They do not run panels. They wander the venue, sit with teams, ask questions, observe the work. One additional partner joins for Saturday and Sunday's afternoon "office hours" where founders can request a thirty-minute conversation in a quiet room.

**Audience composition.** Twenty to thirty founders, selected by application. The VC reads each application. Selection criteria: existing company, evidence of prior shipping (GitHub history, prior employment, prior product launches), early traction signals. Founders need not be raising. Many of the best invitees should be a year or more before their next round.

**Success criterion.** Twelve months after each sprint, what fraction of attendees have either (a) become a portfolio investment for the host fund or (b) raised a round from another credible fund. Floor: `30%`. Stretch: `55%`. Below `25%` the format is not working and the selection criteria need re-examination.

**Kill criterion.** Two consecutive sprints with attendee-to-investment conversion below `1 in 30` indicates the format does not source for this fund. Retire the format.

## Mechanics

- **Venue.** A coworking space or an office (often the VC's own, after hours and on weekends). Capacity for thirty workstations, two private rooms for partner conversations and a kitchen.
- **Budget.** Roughly `$15K to $25K per sprint`. Breakdown: venue ($3 to 8K if rented), food for forty-eight hours ($6 to 10K for thirty people across meals), drinks and snacks, a modest welcome dinner Friday night ($3K), incidentals. The host fund covers all attendee costs. Travel reimbursement up to `$500` per non-local attendee.
- **Decision-making.** Application review by two partners over a two-week window. Final invite list approved by the lead partner. Founders given two weeks' notice between invite and sprint to plan their work.
- **Programming.** Friday evening: arrival, kickoff dinner, partners introduce themselves and the sprint format (`90 minutes` total). Saturday: open work day, ad-hoc partner conversations, optional afternoon "operator office hours" with one or two operators from portfolio companies. Sunday: open work day until 4pm, then a one-hour share-out where each team demos what they shipped, then closing dinner.
- **Documentation.** Internal one-page write-up per attendee within seven days of the sprint, distilling partner observations into a deal-flow record. No external write-up. The sprint is not a marketing event.

## Risks

**Risk: the format selects for founders who can dedicate a weekend, which biases against founders with childcare obligations or other constraints.** Mitigation: offer one virtual track per quarter, structured so founders ship asynchronously over the same forty-eight hours with daily check-ins. Different format, similar selection signal.

**Risk: founders perform for the partners rather than work.** Mitigation: partners explicitly avoid making the sprint feel like an audition. The pre-event communication is clear: this is not a pitch event, partners are not evaluating in the moment, the work itself is the conversation.

**Risk: a partner's casual remark inside the sprint is taken as commitment or as critique by a founder.** Mitigation: partners commit to writing follow-up emails to any founder they spoke with substantively, within seven days, summarizing what they actually meant.

**Risk: the sprint becomes seen as a back-door to the fund's pipeline, and applicants game it.** Mitigation: rotate selection criteria emphasis each year. Some sprints favor open-source contributors, some favor solo technical founders, some favor early-revenue companies. Make it hard to optimize for.

## Open questions

How should the VC handle the founder who attends and was good but the partner does not invest in? Standard practice: a written, specific follow-up explaining what was missing for the fund (not for the founder), with an open door for future rounds. Most founders who attend a sprint and do not get funded should still feel that the time was worth it.

What about founders working on regulated products where shipping in public over a weekend is impossible (medical devices, financial products, hardware)? The "shipping" definition should be flexible. For these founders, the visible progress can be a customer letter signed, a regulatory filing prepared, a manufacturing partner agreement executed. The work is still real and observable, even if not public-internet visible.

Does the format work for fund stages above seed (Series A+)? Probably less well. By Series A, the founders have a team and the founder's individual capacity is no longer the bet. The partner has access to engineering org charts and customer references that contain more signal than a weekend's worth of observation. The sprint format is most powerful at the seed and pre-seed stage where the founder is still doing most of the work personally.

What is the right disclosure for the sprint's outcomes? My read is minimal external disclosure. The fund does not publish attendee names, does not market the program publicly beyond a one-paragraph description on the fund's website and does not announce investments as "originated from build sprint X." The disclosure discipline is what keeps the format honest. Founders attend because the format genuinely helps them, not because attendance is a credential.
