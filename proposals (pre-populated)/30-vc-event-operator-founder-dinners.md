CATEGORY: EIP
TITLE: Host monthly closed-door dinners pairing prospective founders with credible operators

ABSTRACT: VC pitch dinners are vendor events. Founders perform. Operators get nothing in return for their time and stop attending. A different shape, modeled on physician-society practice and Vistage-style peer groups, works better. Eight to ten people per dinner. Half prospective founders, half late-stage operators from credible companies. One specific operational topic. Off-the-record. The VC convenes but does not dominate. The signal the VC takes home is not the founder's pitch but how each side speaks when neither is selling.

BODY:
A VC partner who has hosted thirty pitch dinners can recite the rhythm from memory. Six founders show up. They take turns pitching. The VC asks questions. The other founders nod. Nobody learns anything they did not know walking in. The same dinner, restructured so that operators participate as peers rather than as panelists, produces a different conversation. The founder who would have pitched at table-five-feet stops pitching and starts asking the operator how they handled the exact problem the founder is stuck on. The operator, freed from the obligation to perform credentials, answers honestly. The VC listens.

## What's wrong with the current format

The standard VC dinner is twelve people at a long table in a private room. The VC sits at the head. Founders sit on either side. The VC opens with a few words about the fund. Founders go around the table, sixty seconds each, introducing their company. Conversation breaks into pairs, each founder trying to maximize their face time with the VC. By dessert, three founders have requested follow-up coffees. The VC's signal-to-noise on which founder is actually worth backing sits roughly where it was when the founders walked in. The dinner cost `~$2,400` in food and venue and `~3 hours` of partner time and produced one to two warm-introduction follow-ups, of which `~0.5` typically convert to anything material over the next year.

The operators who could meaningfully evaluate these founders are not in the room because they have not been asked. When operators are in the room (as occasional panelists or speakers), the format puts them in performance mode rather than peer mode and the conversation stays superficial. The thing the VC actually wants to know about a founder is what an operator who has built the founder's category for ten years thinks of them after a real conversation. That conversation does not happen at a pitch dinner.

By my read of VC sourcing data across multiple early-stage funds, dinners as currently structured are a poor sourcing channel, generating `~1 to 2 investments per year per fund` against `~12 to 24 dinners hosted`. The conversion rate per dinner sits well below `1%`. The dinners are kept on the calendar because they are calendar-shaped and partners feel productive while doing them, not because the math works.

## Proposed format

Closed-door monthly dinners with a specific governance shape adapted from peer-learning practice.

**Composition.** Eight to ten people per dinner. Four to five prospective founders. Three to four late-stage operators from credible companies (VPs, heads of function, former operators now investors or advisors). One to two VC partners. No journalists. No marketing or business-development staff.

**Topic.** Each dinner is built around one specific operational topic chosen in advance. Examples: "first ten enterprise sales hires", "the eighteenth-month go-to-market pivot", "what we learned from churn in 2025", "managing the technical-debt curve from prototype to production". The founders in the room are working on companies where the topic is currently the binding constraint. The operators in the room have solved that constraint at scale.

**Off-the-record by default.** Substance can be repeated. Names cannot. The Chatham House rule is stated explicitly at the start of each dinner and reinforced when needed.

**Vendor (VC) role.** The VC's partner sets the topic, selects the attendees and convenes the conversation. The VC does not dominate the conversation, does not pitch the fund, does not solicit decks at the dinner. The VC's job is to listen. The founders learn from operators. The VC learns about the founders by watching them learn.

**Cadence.** Monthly. Same fund partner hosts each dinner. Same private dining room when possible (consistency of venue creates familiarity for repeat attendees, even though most attendees attend only once).

**Success criterion.** Twelve months after each dinner cohort, what fraction of the founder attendees raised a round from a credible fund (the host fund or a peer fund)? Floor: `40%`. Stretch: `65%`. The metric is intentionally not "what fraction became host-fund investments" because the dinner's value to founders comes from the operator conversation, not from being a fund pipeline event, and forcing the founder-to-fund conversion is the failure mode the format is designed to avoid.

**Kill criterion.** If after six dinners the host fund has sourced zero investments and the operator-attendees stop accepting invitations, the format is not working for this fund. Retire it.

## Mechanics

- **Venue.** A consistent private dining room at a credible restaurant. Capacity for ten people around one table. Food at a price point that signals seriousness without ostentation (`~$120 to $200 per person, all in`).
- **Budget.** `~$1,500 to $2,500` per dinner all-in. Annual cost: `~$25 to $35K` for twelve dinners. Roughly the cost of `0.1 of a partner's compensation`.
- **Invitation cadence.** Invitations go out three weeks before each dinner. Operators commit first. Founder invitations follow. Last-minute cancellations are common and the host should plan for `~15 to 20%` no-show rate among founders.
- **Topic selection.** Topic chosen by the host partner six weeks before the dinner, in consultation with one or two operators who have agreed to attend. The topic reflects what is currently most pressing in the host fund's pipeline.
- **Operator selection.** Operators selected for relevance to the topic and for capacity to speak honestly (not all operators can). The host fund's portfolio CEOs and post-exit founders are the natural starting pool. Outside operators (no relationship to the fund) added carefully.
- **Founder selection.** Founders invited based on the host fund's pipeline plus open application (a simple form on the fund's website, reviewed monthly). Mix of pipeline and open-application founders is healthy. Pure-pipeline tends to feel like a sales event.
- **Pre-dinner reading.** One short document (one to three pages) circulated to all attendees twenty-four hours before the dinner, framing the topic and surfacing two or three specific questions. The reading creates a shared starting point and reduces opening small-talk.
- **Documentation.** The host partner writes a one-page summary of the dinner (themes, not attributions) within seven days, shared internally at the fund. No external publication.

## Risks

**Risk: founders cannot stop themselves from pitching the operators.** Mitigation: the host states explicitly at the start of each dinner that pitching is not the purpose of the evening and that any founder who pitches will get a polite mid-dinner redirect. The risk diminishes after a founder has attended one or two operator-attended dinners and learned the norm.

**Risk: operators stop attending because the format feels like work without payment.** Mitigation: operators get value from each other (peer conversation among operators is itself rare and valuable) and from being introduced to the next generation of founders in their category. The host fund also reciprocates: when an operator wants a warm intro to another operator, the host fund makes the intro. Both sides give and take.

**Risk: the conversation stays polite and surface-level because the table is too large.** Mitigation: keep the table at ten people maximum. Larger tables fail. If the topic requires a larger group, host two parallel small tables in adjacent rooms with the host partner spending forty-five minutes in each.

**Risk: a founder reveals competitive information at the dinner and another founder uses it.** Mitigation: the off-the-record rule is contractual through attendance. A founder who breaks the rule is not invited back, and the rest of the operator and founder community hears about it through the host fund's network. The reputational consequence is the enforcement.

## Open questions

How does the format work for funds that are not generalist? Vertical funds (climate, defense, healthcare) can run the same format with topic and operator selection adapted to the vertical. The cohort sizes might be smaller because the operator pool in any one vertical is smaller, but the format scales down to six or seven attendees per dinner without losing value.

What is the right ratio of operator-attendees to founder-attendees? My read is roughly equal. Too many founders and the operators feel outnumbered and revert to performance mode. Too many operators and the founders feel evaluated rather than included. Forty-five percent founders, forty-five percent operators, ten percent host fund staff is the rough balance.

When does the format saturate? Operators are a finite pool, and the same operator should not attend more than two or three dinners per year before the format feels like work to them. After two years of monthly dinners, the host fund will have rotated through roughly seventy operators, which is the meaningful pool for any one ecosystem. Beyond that, the format either evolves (different operator pools, different topics) or contracts (fewer dinners per year, same operators).

Should the dinners ever go on the record? Probably not. The format's value depends on the discretion guarantee. A single "special edition" on-the-record dinner per year for narrow strategic purposes (a publicly-disclosable industry summit) is possible, but it is a different event running under different rules and should be marketed and named differently.

How does this scale to multiple cities? A multi-city fund can run parallel monthly dinners in two or three cities with different partners hosting each. The format is the same. The operator pool and the topic mix shifts per city. Coordination between cities is light. Each city's dinners are sized to the local pipeline.
