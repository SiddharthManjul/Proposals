CATEGORY: EIP
TITLE: Split the First Round CEO Summit into quarterly fifty-person micro-summits

ABSTRACT: The First Round CEO Summit is the gold standard of VC-hosted portfolio events. It is also outgrowing the format that made it the gold standard. The annual event runs `~250 to 300 attendees` and increasingly resembles a conference rather than a peer-learning gathering. The wedge is to keep the annual flagship but add quarterly fifty-person micro-summits clustered by company stage and theme, hosted at smaller venues with mandatory off-the-record rules and structured peer-cohort discussions rather than mainstage programming. The annual event keeps the brand. The quarterly micro-summits restore the peer-learning depth.

BODY:
The First Round CEO Summit is unusual among VC events because it actually works. Founders fly across the country for it. Partners hold three years of board-meeting capital in reserve to attend. The content is genuinely useful and the relationships formed are real. The format has been refined over more than a decade and the cohort is exceptional.

The format has also stopped scaling. At three hundred attendees, the room is no longer a peer cohort. The conversations break into fragments. The mainstage programming dominates the time. The thing the founders most want (an honest peer conversation about the specific problem they are stuck on) is rarer than it was when the event was smaller.

## What's wrong with the current format

The Summit has grown by `~3 to 4x` in attendee count over the last decade, mostly because First Round's portfolio has grown. The event currently runs as a multi-day off-site at a destination property with a mix of mainstage talks, breakout sessions, networking events and structured small-group conversations. Attendees report that the relationships formed at the event are valuable but that the depth of those relationships has thinned over time. The phrase one hears repeatedly is "it used to be the most useful three days of my year, and now it is one of three or four most useful three days."

By my read of the format's evolution and the comparable scaling experience of similar peer-learning communities (YPO Forum, the Vistage groups, the EO experience), the breakpoint is around eighty to one hundred attendees. Beyond that, the room stops being a single peer cohort and becomes a conference. The conversations that mattered when the event was small (founders telling each other things they would not tell their boards) become harder as the attendee count grows, because the trust premise that allows those conversations does not extend to three hundred people the founder does not personally know.

The cost of the Summit, both in fund operating budget and in partner-time, is substantial. By my count, an event of this scale at a destination property costs in the range of `~$1M to $2M per year` in direct expenses plus `~12 to 20 partner-weeks` of preparation time. The cost per attendee runs `~$3,500 to $6,000` once all expenses are accounted for. The value the attendees get is real but the marginal value per attendee has been declining as the cohort has scaled past the size where peer learning works.

The alumni of earlier Summits often describe the event from `~2014 to 2019` as the formative event of their CEO experience. The current era's attendees describe it as "excellent" or "important," which is praise but not the same praise. The format change has cost something even as the brand has compounded.

## Proposed format

Keep the annual flagship Summit (it is the brand-defining event and the alumni connection is preserved by it). Add a quarterly micro-summit cadence with smaller cohorts and stricter peer-learning discipline.

**Annual flagship.** Existing shape preserved at three hundred attendees. One per year. Continues to serve as the alumni reunion, the partner showcase, the network-density event. Programming and format unchanged.

**Quarterly micro-summits.** New. Four per year. Each runs fifty attendees maximum. Cohort selection is theme- and stage-based. Examples of micro-summit themes: "Series A CEOs in vertical SaaS", "first-time CEOs at Series B or earlier", "second-time founders with one prior exit", "CEOs navigating an acquisition decision". Each theme runs once per year.

**Cadence.** Quarterly. Four micro-summits per year. Each summit is two days, in a small venue (a private property, a boutique hotel block, occasionally a portfolio CEO's own facility).

**Composition.** Fifty CEOs per micro-summit. Sequenced to ensure peer-cohort density. Each attendee should have at least eight other attendees they could plausibly form a "founder peer group" with after the event. Theme selection enforces this density.

**Format.** Day one: structured peer-cohort conversations in groups of eight to ten, on topics the attendees submitted in advance. Day two: shared sessions across cohorts, plus one to two operating-partner-led workshops, plus a closing dinner. No mainstage keynotes. No external speakers.

**Off-the-record rules.** Chatham House throughout both days. No recording, no journalists, no portfolio-marketing extraction. The rules are written down and signed before attendance.

**Host shape.** Two First Round partners attend each micro-summit, in participant role rather than as the program's center. The partners are explicitly not the keynote. They are in the small-group conversations as peers.

**Success criterion.** Six months after each micro-summit, attendees report (in a structured follow-up survey) on whether the relationships formed at the summit are still active. Healthy: `>70%` report at least two ongoing peer relationships from the cohort. Below `50%` indicates the summit composition was wrong.

**Kill criterion.** Two consecutive micro-summits with attendee NPS below `40` or with cohort cohesion below the `50%` floor. Pause that theme. Re-examine selection criteria before re-running.

## Mechanics

- **Application or invitation.** Half of each micro-summit's seats are direct invitation from First Round partners based on portfolio relationships and stage match. Half are open application by the broader portfolio. Selection committee balances seniority, stage, vertical and geography within each theme.
- **Venue.** Small venues (under one hundred guest rooms) selected per micro-summit. Some at established meeting properties. Some at portfolio-CEO-hosted properties (where a portfolio CEO's company owns or runs a venue and wants to host).
- **Budget.** `~$200K to $400K per micro-summit` all-in. Quarterly: `~$1M to $1.6M per year`. Combined with the annual flagship: total program budget runs roughly `$2M to $4M per year`, roughly double the current Summit budget. Justified by the increased portfolio engagement and the differential depth of the relationships formed.
- **Theme selection.** Annually, four themes selected by the Summit program team in consultation with a rotating "CEO Council" of past Summit attendees. Themes are concrete and stage-specific, not abstract ("scaling go-to-market" is too vague. "Series A to Series B in vertical SaaS" is right).
- **Cohort matching.** Within each theme, attendees pre-cluster into peer groups of eight to ten before the summit. The clusters are sent to each attendee one week ahead so they arrive knowing who they will be in deepest conversation with. This is the structural move that converts a fifty-person gathering into five peer cohorts of ten.
- **Documentation.** Internal one-paragraph summary per cohort by the partner host. No external summary. The summit is for the attendees, not for marketing.
- **Smallest viable version.** Run one micro-summit alongside the existing annual flagship in the first year. Measure cohort cohesion and follow-on engagement. Scale to four micro-summits the following year if the data supports it.

## Risks

**Risk: founders who only attend the annual flagship feel demoted to a less-valuable tier.** Mitigation: the framing is explicit. The flagship is the brand-and-network event. The micro-summits are the peer-learning event. Different products, both valuable. CEOs who want both attend both.

**Risk: the quarterly cadence cannibalizes the annual flagship and attendance at the flagship drops.** Mitigation: track flagship attendance over the first three years of the micro-summit cadence. If flagship attendance falls below `~80% of current`, reconsider the micro-summit cadence (perhaps reduce to two per year). The flagship is the institutional anchor and protecting it matters.

**Risk: micro-summit themes proliferate without discipline and each summit becomes too specialized.** Mitigation: four themes per year is the budget. Themes selected for breadth-of-applicability rather than narrowness. A theme that only matters to fifteen portfolio CEOs is too narrow. The cohort cannot reach fifty attendees in that case.

**Risk: confidentiality breaks under the larger summit volume.** Mitigation: each micro-summit's Chatham House rules are repeated at the start, attendees sign explicit commitments and the consequence for breaches is exclusion from future micro-summits plus removal from the broader First Round CEO network. The reputational consequence is the enforcement.

**Risk: the operational overhead of running five summits per year (one flagship plus four micro-summits) overwhelms the program team.** Mitigation: the program team scales with the cadence. Two to three dedicated operations staff for the combined program. The marginal operational cost of a fifty-person event is materially lower than the per-attendee cost of a three-hundred-person event.

## Open questions

How does the micro-summit interact with founder peer groups that already exist among the portfolio? Probably composes well. The micro-summit is a quarterly anchor. The peer groups continue between summits. The summit functions as the gathering surface where new peer groups form and old ones reconnect.

What about non-CEO attendees (CFOs, CTOs, heads of revenue)? My read is the micro-summit format works better for non-CEO functional leaders than the annual flagship currently does. A "CFOs at Series B" micro-summit, a "CTOs at vertical SaaS Series A" micro-summit, etc. The format scales naturally into functional cohorts in addition to CEO cohorts. Worth piloting in year two.

How does First Round preserve the flagship's institutional weight if the most-valuable conversations are happening at the micro-summits? The flagship's role evolves toward alumni reunion and network density (the strongest part of the current format) rather than peer learning (which migrates to the micro-summits). Both functions are valuable. The flagship's value proposition is not threatened, it is clarified.

What about prospective portfolio companies (CEOs of companies First Round has not invested in but might)? My read is they do not attend micro-summits. The micro-summits are portfolio-only. The annual flagship can occasionally invite prospective founders. Mixing the two audiences in the micro-summit format breaks the trust premise.

How does this scale if First Round's portfolio doubles over the next five years? The cadence scales: more themes per year, more micro-summits per theme. Or cohort sizes stay at fifty and selection becomes more competitive. Both are viable. Probably some of each, decided as the portfolio grows.
