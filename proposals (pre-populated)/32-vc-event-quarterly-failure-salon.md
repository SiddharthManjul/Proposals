CATEGORY: EIP
TITLE: Host a quarterly closed-door failure salon where founders of shut-down companies present what happened

ABSTRACT: Founder Twitter sanitizes failure. The thoughtful post-mortems get rewritten as "what I learned" essays that resolve every story into a lesson, which is exactly the shape that prevents the next generation of founders from learning the actual hard things. A quarterly closed-door salon, off the record, where one founder of a recently failed company presents the full story to twenty other founders, with no press and no recording, produces the kind of honest failure analysis that the public ecosystem actively suppresses. The VC host gains relationships built on the rare trust of holding the room and not extracting it for marketing.

BODY:
A founder whose company died last quarter is not going to write a blog post about why. They might write one in eighteen months once the equity is gone and the lawyers are quiet, but it will be retrofitted into a redemption narrative that the next generation of founders cannot use. The honest version exists only in private conversations. The format that captures it is older than venture capital itself.

## What's wrong with the current format

The default founder-to-founder learning surface in 2026 is Twitter, Substack and conference panels. All three optimize against honest failure analysis. Twitter rewards the punchline. The failure story compresses into a thread that performs as content. Substack rewards the redemption arc. The founder structures the failure as preparation for the next thing. Conference panels reward the polished narrative. The host fund or the conference organizer wants the failure story but reframed as resilience or as lesson.

By my read of public failure post-mortems over the last three years, roughly `~85%` follow a recognizable structure: it started well, the market shifted, we made hard decisions, here is what we learned, on to the next thing. The structure is not dishonest. It is incomplete. The parts that are missing: the months when the founder knew the company was failing but did not yet say it, the conversations with co-founders that ended relationships, the moments of considering fraud or pivoting into territory the founder did not believe in, the specific mistakes the founder made that the founder still has not fully forgiven themselves for. These parts are the ones the next generation of founders most needs to hear, and they are systematically suppressed by every public surface.

The founders who attempt to share these parts in public face a real reputational cost. Future fundraises get harder. Future hires hesitate. The founder learns within one tweet cycle that honesty is punished, even when the audience claims to want it. The result is a learning surface that is dense in content and thin in signal.

## Proposed format

A quarterly evening salon, closed door, where one founder of a recently failed company presents the full story of what happened to twenty other founders.

**Duration.** Three hours total. Ninety minutes of presentation and Q&A from the failed-company founder. Ninety minutes of conversation among attendees after the founder has finished (during which the founder can stay or leave. Both have happened in similar formats and both work).

**Cadence.** Quarterly. Four salons per year. Each salon features a different failed founder. Rotating cities (typically two SF, one NYC, one rotating among Boston, LA, Austin).

**Composition.** One failed founder, twenty current founders. No press. No investors other than the host (and the host is in their personal capacity, not their fund-affiliation capacity, during the conversation). No recording. No phones at the table. The room is small enough that everyone is visible.

**Off-the-record by Chatham House.** Substance can be discussed in private. Names cannot be quoted. The failed founder's company name is shared inside the room. Outside the room, attendees are not to identify which founder presented.

**Failed-founder selection.** The host approaches founders of companies that have shut down or made the public transition to dormant within the prior twelve to twenty-four months. The founder must want to do this. Nobody is pressured. The host's reputation among prior presenters becomes the recruiting tool over time.

**Attendee selection.** Twenty founders, invited by the host. Mix of stages from pre-seed to Series B, currently operating. No journalists, no employees of large companies attending as future founders, no podcast hosts. The selection criterion is "founder who is currently in the work and who can hold a confidence."

**Success criterion.** Net Promoter Score among attendees post-salon. Healthy: NPS above `60`. Repeat-attendee rate (attendees who request to attend a second salon within twelve months): `>75%`. Below those, the format is not delivering what attendees say they want.

**Indirect criterion.** Twelve months after each salon, what fraction of attendees raise a round in which the host fund participates, in which the salon was the qualifying introduction? Floor: `15%`. Stretch: `30%`. The fund does not market the salon as a sourcing channel, but the sourcing effect is real and the fund tracks it internally.

**Kill criterion.** Two consecutive salons with NPS below `30` or with no founder willing to present indicates the format has lost its credibility. Pause and re-examine.

## Mechanics

- **Venue.** A private dining room or a quiet hosted space. The same venue per city when possible. Modest food and one round of drinks before the presentation begins. Phones collected at the door (literally, in a basket) and returned on exit.
- **Budget.** `~$3K to $5K per salon`. Annual cost: `~$15 to $20K`. The cost is mostly food, venue and travel for the presenting founder if needed.
- **Presenting founder honorarium.** The presenting founder receives `$2,000` for their preparation and presentation. The honorarium is not the reason they present. The reason is that the format lets them say things they cannot say publicly and that they need to say to someone. The honorarium acknowledges the work without commercializing it.
- **Pre-event preparation.** The host meets with the presenting founder one or two times before the salon to help them frame the story. The presenting founder is not coached on what to say. They are helped to plan a ninety-minute structure that gives them room to cover the full arc, including the parts they are still working on. The host's role is structural, not editorial.
- **Documentation.** No formal documentation. Attendees may take handwritten notes for personal use. No transcript, no recording, no slide deck shared after. The host writes a private one-paragraph internal note for fund records, with no quotable content.
- **No host fund branding in the room.** The salon happens at the host's invitation and is associated with the host's personal name (or the partner's). The fund's branding is not on the invitation, not in the room, not in any follow-up communication. Attendees know which fund the host is part of, but the fund is not the brand of the evening.
- **Smallest viable version.** Run two salons before scaling to quarterly cadence. If the second salon produces conversation quality the host has not heard before and presenting founders willing to do this work, scale to the full cadence.

## Risks

**Risk: confidentiality breaks.** An attendee tweets a fragment from the salon or describes it on a podcast. The presenting founder's trust is damaged and future presenters refuse. Mitigation: clear rules at the door, written commitment signed by attendees, reputational consequences for breaches (the breaching attendee is not invited again and the founder community hears about it). The format has been run in adjacent contexts (the EO Forum, Reboot's group format, the Vistage equivalent at later-stage) and confidentiality holds reliably when the rules are stated.

**Risk: the presenting founder gets re-traumatized.** Mitigation: the host pre-screens for emotional readiness. Founders who are still in the immediate aftermath of failure (less than three months) generally should not present. The format works best for founders who are six to eighteen months past shut-down, with enough distance to speak coherently but not enough to have rewritten the story.

**Risk: the format is perceived as ghoulish or exploitative of the failed founder.** Mitigation: the format is invitation-only, the founder is paid, the format gives the founder a controlled audience for content they could not say in public and the discretion is real. Founders who present typically report it as one of the more meaningful evenings of their post-shutdown period. The risk is real but the mitigation works.

**Risk: the format becomes a fund-marketing channel and attendees notice.** Mitigation: the fund's brand is not on the evening. The host attends in personal capacity. Any deal that comes from a salon attendee is a separate conversation in a different setting. Keeping the salon non-commercial is the discipline that makes it possible.

**Risk: only certain demographics of failed founders are comfortable presenting.** Mitigation: the host actively invites a diverse set of failed founders, including ones who are not in the most-visible failure-narrative pool. Diversity here is partly about who gets to tell their story, partly about which failure modes the audience hears about.

## Open questions

Should there ever be on-the-record salons? Probably not, or extremely rarely. The format depends on the discretion guarantee. A separate event series, distinguished by name and rules, could be on-the-record. The salon should remain off-the-record permanently.

How does the host handle a salon where the presenting founder says something legally exposed (admitting fraud, naming a still-employed person at a still-operating company in a damaging way)? The host's role includes a light editorial check during pre-event preparation. Some content should not be said even in a closed room. The host can flag this to the founder in advance. After the event, if something was said that should not have been, the host's discretion to never repeat it is the protection.

What about the host's fund colleagues? Can other partners attend? My read is no. The format depends on small numbers and on the host's personal reliability. If a second partner attends, they should attend as a fellow listener under the same rules, but the salon should not become a fund-sourced event.

What happens when failed founders become a recognized pipeline? Some founders will attempt to present at salons as a way to be socially seen, particularly if the format gains reputation. The host's curation is the defense. Not every failed founder gets invited to present. Selection is based on the depth and honesty of the story the founder has to tell, not on the company's prior valuation or the fund's relationship with the founder.

How does this format compose with the fund's other sourcing channels? Cleanly. Salon attendees overlap with the fund's pipeline incidentally. The salon is not a sourcing channel in design but functions as one in effect. The fund tracks but does not advertise the conversion.
