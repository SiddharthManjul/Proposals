CATEGORY: CMIP
TITLE: Run quarterly peer-cohort circles with off-the-record rules for vertical SaaS practitioner communities

ABSTRACT: Vertical SaaS founders try to build community through vendor-hosted webinars and Slack workspaces that customers attend politely and learn nothing from. The thing the vertical's practitioners actually want is honest conversation with peers about operational problems they cannot discuss with their direct competitors at trade shows. The wedge is structured: small quarterly peer-cohort circles (eight to twelve operators per cohort), facilitated but not led by the vendor, run under explicit off-the-record rules, with rotation across cohorts. The vendor benefits from being the trusted convener and learns more about its customers than the customer-success function ever produces.

BODY:
Operators in any vertical (dental practices, restaurants, contractors, ag operators, fleet managers) have a shared problem the vendor sells against and another shared problem the vendor cannot solve: they have no one to talk to about the operational work. Their direct competitors are in town. Their non-competitors are in other states and they have no easy way to find each other. The conferences are full of vendor pitches. The Facebook groups are noisy and shallow. The thing they would actually pay for is a small, structured peer conversation with operators outside their geography who have solved the problem they are currently stuck on.

## What's broken

The default vertical SaaS community offering is one of two shapes. The first is a webinar series: monthly, vendor-presented, `~120 registrants`, `~45 attend live`, `~12 finish, `~3 ask questions`. The format optimizes for the vendor's marketing metrics (registrants captured, leads generated) and against the customer's actual learning (operators rarely speak, the speakers are vendor staff or vendor-selected customers). Operators attend once or twice, learn nothing they did not already know and stop attending.

The second shape is a vendor-hosted Slack or community forum. Operators post questions. Vendor employees answer them within an hour. Other operators rarely answer. By my count, in a typical `~400-member` vendor community Slack, peer-to-peer responses run at `~12 to 18%` of total responses. The rest is vendor-to-customer. Operators ask once or twice and learn that the format is for vendor support, not peer learning, and gravitate back to the Facebook groups where at least the responses are unbiased even if shallow.

Neither shape produces the thing operators say they want when asked directly. In customer surveys across multiple vertical SaaS companies, "learning from other operators" ranks in the top three customer requests at `~58 to 72%` weighting, while "vendor webinars" ranks near the bottom at `~6 to 11%`. The gap between what the customer asks for and what the vendor delivers is the opportunity.

## Proposed change

Stand up quarterly peer-cohort circles. Specific structure:

**Cohort size: eight to twelve operators.** Larger than that and individual airtime drops below useful. Smaller and absences (which happen) hurt the dynamic.

**Non-competing composition.** Operators in the same vertical but not competing for the same customers. Geographic separation is the typical mechanic (no two operators within a `~50-mile radius` for local businesses. No two operators serving the same end-customer for B2B operators). Composition decision made by the vendor based on customer relationships.

**Quarterly meetings.** Two hours each. Four meetings per year. Same cohort across all four meetings (annual cohorts), then rotate composition.

**Off-the-record by default.** Chatham House rule. Substance discussable outside the meeting. Specific operators not quotable by name. Confidentiality includes vendor staff. The vendor does not use cohort-meeting content for marketing.

**Vendor-facilitated, not vendor-led.** Vendor staff member attends as facilitator: sets the agenda with operator input, manages time, takes notes for the cohort's own use. Vendor staff do not present material, do not steer the conversation toward product topics and do not respond to vendor-related questions in the meeting (those go to a written follow-up).

**Operator-driven agenda.** Each cohort surfaces topics two weeks before the meeting. Cohort votes on priority. The two highest-voted topics structure the meeting. Vendor adds context but does not set the agenda.

**Annual graduation.** After four meetings together, a cohort either re-forms with new composition or graduates and joins a "graduated peer network" with looser ad-hoc structure. New cohorts form annually.

What changes versus today: the format (small, peer-to-peer, facilitator-not-presenter), the protection (off-the-record, vendor commits to not extracting marketing material), the agenda (operator-driven), the recurrence (cohort persists through a year of meetings) and the vendor's role (convener, not center).

## What this is and isn't

This is a peer-learning structure modeled on professional-society practice (the YPO/Vistage-style format adapted for one vertical's operators with a vendor as the convening sponsor). It is not a customer-marketing program and it is not a vendor advisory board. Vendor-marketing extraction from cohort meetings breaks the format inside one cycle.

This is not a substitute for direct customer-success interaction. Customer-success operations run in parallel, with the cohort program as a separate and additive offering. Operators in the cohort still have their account manager, their support escalation path and their normal vendor touchpoints. The cohort is for peer learning, not for vendor support.

This is not a closed club for the vendor's biggest customers. Cohort selection should reflect the customer base's diversity, including smaller operators who would benefit most. Vendor-friendly customers can serve as cohort facilitators or alumni members of a graduated network, which preserves their special access without distorting the cohort composition itself.

## Mechanics

- **Selection.** Vendor identifies candidates from the customer base based on operational maturity (typically `~6+ months` as a customer with active product use) and openness signals (responsive to outreach, attended past vendor events without being there for marketing reasons). Operators opt in with a one-page commitment to attendance and to the off-the-record rule. First cohort: two cohorts of ten operators each, formed concurrently.
- **Facilitator.** A vendor staff member with operational experience in the vertical (former dental practice manager, former restaurant operator, former contractor). Not a marketing or sales person. The facilitator is the vendor's most trusted listener.
- **Meeting cadence.** Quarterly. `~2 hours` per meeting. Virtual (Zoom) by default. One in-person meeting per year if budget allows.
- **Confidentiality enforcement.** Written agreement signed by each operator and by the vendor. Specific provisions: no recording, no quotes attributed by name outside the meeting, vendor cannot use cohort-derived content in marketing without explicit per-content consent.
- **Cost.** Facilitator time: `~6 hours/cohort/quarter` (`~24 hours/year per cohort`). At facilitator loaded cost of `~$100/hr`, that's `~$2,400/cohort/year`. Plus venue (zero for virtual, `~$2,000` for one annual in-person session). Two cohorts of ten operators: roughly `~$10K/year` all-in. The vendor's cost per operator is roughly `$500/year`.
- **Annual report.** End-of-year written summary of themes from cohort meetings, redacted to remove specific operator attributions. Shared with the broader customer community and with the company's product team. The themes inform the vendor's roadmap.
- **Smallest viable version.** Start with one cohort of eight operators. Run two meetings. If the second meeting produces honest peer-to-peer conversation about operational topics the vendor was not aware of, scale to two cohorts. If the second meeting stays polite and surface-level, the composition or the facilitation was wrong.
- **Traction metric.** Cohort attendance rate. Healthy: `>85%` per meeting. Below `70%` suggests the meetings are not delivering value.
- **Retention metric.** Annual renewal rate of cohort members. Operators who participated in a cohort renew their vendor subscription at materially higher rates than the customer base average. The lift is roughly `~6 to 12 percentage points` based on the limited data we have from companies that have run this format for two or more years.

## Open questions

What about competitive operators who do not want to discuss operational details with peers? Most operators in vertical-SaaS customer bases are not the size or scale to perceive other geographic-non-competing operators as threats. The exception is operators in highly-consolidated verticals where everyone is in everyone else's growth path. For those verticals, the cohort format works only if the composition is unusually rigorous about non-competition or if it shifts to a single-operator-per-cohort consulting format.

What about regulatory exposure? The off-the-record rule is contractual, not legal-privileged. Operators should not discuss anything that would constitute an antitrust violation (price coordination, market division). The facilitator's role includes steering the conversation away from these topics if they emerge. Most operational topics (workflow, hiring, vendor relationships, regulatory compliance) are safe.

When does the vendor's role become a conflict? When the vendor's commercial interests directly oppose what the cohort is discussing (a competitor's product, a pricing concern about the vendor's own offerings). The vendor's commitment to off-the-record extends to its own commercial interests: the cohort can discuss anything without fearing the vendor will respond commercially. This is hard to maintain in practice and is the main reason vendor-led cohort programs fail in their second year.

How does this scale to a customer base above `~1,000 customers`? With more cohorts, not larger cohorts. Cohort size stays at eight to twelve regardless of customer-base size. A vendor with `~3,000 active customers` might run six concurrent cohorts. Resource scaling is roughly linear with cohort count.

What about international customer bases? Cohorts can be regional (English-speaking operators across multiple time zones) or single-country. Cross-cultural cohorts work for verticals where operational practices are similar across regions. They fail for verticals where local regulation dominates the operational reality.
